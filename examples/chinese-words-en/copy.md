# Everyday English words that came from Chinese: ketchup, tyco

## Idea

Four everyday English words (ketchup, tycoon, typhoon, kowtow) have roots in Chinese. Each one hides a surprising original meaning.

## Hook

You speak a little Chinese already. Four everyday English words started there.

## Copy

Ketchup: it started as a fermented fish sauce, likely from the Hokkien 'kê-tsiap'. Tomatoes came much later.

Tycoon: the Chinese 'dà jūn', 'great lord', reached English through the Japanese 'taikun', a title used for the shogun.

Typhoon: partly the Chinese 'big wind' (Cantonese 'daai fung'), blended with the Greek storm monster Typhon and the Arabic 'tufan'.

Kowtow: from 'kòu tóu', literally 'knock head', meaning to bow until your forehead touches the ground.

Four words, one language behind them. Which one surprised you?

## Facts

- Ketchup likely comes from a Hokkien (Amoy) Chinese word for a brine or sauce of pickled fish, possibly by way of Malay 'kecap'. (source: Online Etymology Dictionary, 'ketchup', https://www.etymonline.com/word/ketchup; Merriam-Webster, 'ketchup', https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/ketchup)
- Tycoon comes from Japanese 'taikun', a title for the shogun used with foreigners, from Chinese 'great' + 'prince/lord' (大君). (source: Online Etymology Dictionary, 'tycoon', https://www.etymonline.com/word/tycoon)
- Typhoon is partly from Chinese 'great wind' (Cantonese 'tai fung'), merged with Arabic 'tufan' and Greek 'Typhon'. (source: Online Etymology Dictionary, 'typhoon', https://www.etymonline.com/word/typhoon)
- Kowtow comes from Chinese 'k'o-t'ou' (叩頭), from 'knock' + 'head', the act of touching the forehead to the ground in respect. (source: Online Etymology Dictionary, 'kowtow', https://www.etymonline.com/word/kowtow)
