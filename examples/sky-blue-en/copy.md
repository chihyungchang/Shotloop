# Why is the sky blue, and why are sunsets red

## Idea

The sky is blue and sunsets are red for the same reason: the molecules of the air scatter short blue wavelengths far more than long red ones (Rayleigh scattering), and at sunset light crosses so much more air that even more of the blue is scattered away before it reaches us.

## Hook

Same Sun, same air. So why is the sky blue at noon and red at sunset?

## Copy

Sunlight looks white, but it carries every colour, from short blue waves to long red ones.

When it hits the atmosphere, the tiny molecules of the air scatter it. Short wavelengths scatter far more: blue light about five times more than red. That scattered blue comes at you from every direction, so the whole sky glows blue.

At sunset the Sun is low, and its light crosses dozens of times more air to reach you. Along that long path even more of the blue is scattered away. What gets through is orange and red.

Same air, different path: blue by day, red at dusk.

## Facts

- Sunlight carries every colour. Quoted: "The white light coming from the Sun is really made up of all the colors of the rainbow." (source: NOAA NESDIS SciJinks, "Why Is the Sky Blue?", 31 August 2025, https://www.nesdis.noaa.gov/about/k-12-education/atmosphere/why-the-sky-blue)
- Short wavelengths scatter more, and this is called Rayleigh scattering. Quoted: "This scattering, called Rayleigh scattering, is more effective at short wavelengths (the blue end of the visible spectrum)." The page gives Rayleigh's scattered intensity as a formula, I ∝ 1/λ⁴; the formula is an image on the page, not text. (source: HyperPhysics, Georgia State University, "Blue Sky and Rayleigh Scattering", https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/atmos/blusky.html)
- Blue light is about 470 nm and red about 700 nm. Quoted: a "Wavelengths for ROYGBIV" table lists Blue as "470nm", which HyperPhysics calls "a general guide and not a precise statement about color"; NASA: "red has the longest wavelength, at around 700 nanometers." (source: HyperPhysics, Georgia State University, "Color", https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/vision/specol.html; NASA Science, "Visible Light", https://science.nasa.gov/ems/09_visiblelight/)
- Blue is scattered about 5 times more than red. Our arithmetic from the 1/λ⁴ law, not a quote: (700/470)⁴ = 4.92, shown as "≈5×". (source: arithmetic on HyperPhysics' 1/λ⁴ law, https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/atmos/blusky.html)
- Scattered blue reaches us from all directions, so the whole sky looks blue. Quoted: "Blue light is scattered in all directions by the tiny molecules of air in Earth's atmosphere." (source: NASA Space Place, "Why Is the Sky Blue?", updated 29 August 2022, https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/blue-sky/en/)
- At the horizon, sunlight crosses about 38 times as much air as straight overhead. Quoted: "reaching a value of approximately 38 at the horizon." The air mass is 1 at the zenith by definition. (source: F. Kasten and A. T. Young, "Revised optical air mass tables and approximation formula", Applied Optics 28:22, 4735–4738, 1989, quoted via the Wikipedia article on astronomical air mass, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_mass_%28astronomy%29)
- At sunset, even more blue is scattered away and red gets through. Quoted: "As the Sun gets lower in the sky, its light is passing through more of the atmosphere to reach you. Even more of the blue light is scattered, allowing the reds and yellows to pass straight through to your eyes." (source: NASA Space Place, "Why Is the Sky Blue?", updated 29 August 2022, https://spaceplace.nasa.gov/blue-sky/en/)
